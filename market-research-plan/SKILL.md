# Market Research Planning Skill

## Who This Is For

**User:** 

**Research philosophy:** Exploratory first, confirmatory second. Always understand the "why" before measuring the "what." Literature must inform design before a single question is written.

**Audience for outputs:** Ranges from academic professors to CEOs to interviewers running sessions. Documents must work for all of them — rigorous enough for academia, readable enough for a business executive, practical enough for a field researcher.

**Voice standard for all written outputs:** Expert level but accessible. No jargon without explanation. A non-specialist should feel informed and smart after reading it, not excluded.

---

## The Non-Negotiables (Never Skip These)

1. **Always do a gray paper literature review first** — before writing a single research question, search for what academic and industry research already exists on the topic. Identify what is known, what is contested, and crucially, what gap exists that this study will fill. The gap is the contribution.
2. **Research question must be open, not leading** — never embed the expected finding in the question. "Tensions," "struggles," "challenges" are banned from research questions unless the study has already established they exist.
3. **Qualitative before quantitative** — in a two-phase study, always design the qualitative study first. Themes from interviews feed into survey scale design. Never reverse this.
4. **Every output must state what the research will do for the business** — research without a business case is academic exercise. Always connect findings to product decisions, positioning, segmentation, or strategy.
5. **Documents must be audience-layered** — the same document is read by an interviewer running sessions AND a CEO evaluating investment. Both must find what they need.

## Phase Structure: How to Approach Any Research Project

### Step 1 — Understand the Intuition
Before touching methodology, extract the raw intuition from the user. What have they observed? What shift, behaviour, or tension made them want to do this research? The best research starts with a sharp human observation, not a research gap.

Ask: What did you notice? What made you think something had changed? What are you trying to understand that you can't answer from data you already have?

### Step 2 — Gray Paper Literature Review
Search for existing academic and industry research on the topic. Structure the review as a business case, not an academic exercise. Sections should cover:
- The core tension or paradox the research is entering
- What existing studies have found (with real citations and links)
- Where those studies fall short or haven't looked
- The specific gap this new study will fill

Always include real, verifiable references with URLs. Do not fabricate citations. If a claim can't be sourced, label it clearly as a research hypothesis to be tested.

Output format: gray paper structured as a business document, not an academic paper. Written for a smart generalist, not a specialist.

### Step 3 — Scope the Research
Before writing questions, lock three things:

**The phenomenon:** What exact behaviour, emotion, or pattern are we studying? Be specific enough that you could recognise it in an interview transcript.

**The population:** Who are we studying? Define inclusion criteria but don't over-specify. Let variation emerge rather than designing it in.

**The method:** Qualitative (interviews) for unexplored emotional/social phenomena. Quantitative (survey) for validating patterns at scale. Mixed for both. Never use a survey to study something you don't yet have language for.

### Step 4 — Research Question Design
The research question is the most important sentence in the study. It must be:
- Open: does not presuppose the direction or valence of the finding
- Bounded: specific enough that a researcher knows what counts as an answer
- Researchable: answerable through the method you're using
- Relevant: connected to a real business or knowledge gap

**Banned words in research questions:** tensions, struggles, challenges, problems, difficulties (unless already established by prior literature)

**Good structure:** "How do [population] [verb that implies active navigation or experience] [phenomenon] in [context]?"

Always build 3 sub-questions that map to distinct dimensions of the main question. Each sub-question should be answerable independently and should collectively cover the full scope of the main question.

### Step 5 — Method Design

#### If Qualitative (Semi-Structured Interviews):

**Opening script:** Always use Option B — broad framing that does not reveal the specific phenomenon under study. Let participants name the thing themselves. The opening must establish psychological safety, explain recording and anonymity, and set the conversational tone.

**Question structure:** 12–15 questions across 4–5 thematic sections. Sections should follow a natural narrative arc:
- Warm-up (establish context, no sensitive topics yet)
- The journey (narrative arc of the experience being studied)
- The specific phenomenon (enter indirectly through adjacent topics, then focus)
- The output or consequence (what happens after, what they do with it)
- The social/external dimension (how others are involved, perceived norms)

**Question quality rules:**
- Every question must be open-ended — no yes/no answers possible
- No leading questions — do not embed the expected answer in the phrasing
- No double-barrelled questions — one thing at a time
- Anchor in specific past experience, not hypotheticals — "tell me about the last time" produces richer data than "imagine if"
- Questions that ask for stories produce better data than questions that ask for opinions

**Listening cues:** Every question must include a note on what the researcher is listening for — not just the topic but the emotional signal, the behaviour, the contradiction they should catch.

**Probes:** Every question must have 2–3 follow-up probes ready for when the participant gives a surface-level answer. Probes should open, not direct. The echo technique (repeating the participant's last phrase as a question) is always available.

**Indirect AI probing:** When studying AI-related behaviour, never ask about AI in the first half of the interview. Let it surface naturally. Only introduce it directly if it hasn't appeared by the midpoint. Use tool-first framing: "what tools did you use?" before "did you use AI?"

**Closing question:** Always end with an open invitation for what wasn't asked. "Is there anything we didn't ask about that you think is important?" — this question consistently produces unexpected findings.

**Demographic questions:** Ask at the end, not the beginning. Keep them relevant to the study's interpretive needs. Standard set: role/title, industry, years of experience, task frequency (relevant to study topic), AI/technology usage, age range, gender.

#### If Quantitative (Survey):

**Design order:** Qualitative themes must exist before survey scales are designed. Never build a survey on untested assumptions about what matters.

**Scale standard:** Likert 1–5 unless justified otherwise. Always test for reliability (Cronbach's Alpha). Composite variables should be means of item groups.

**Data cleaning rules:** Maximum 20% missing values per respondent. Reverse-coded items must be flagged and recoded before analysis. Remove preview/test entries before analysis.

**Analysis approach:**
- Descriptive statistics first (means, SDs, distributions)
- Reliability analysis before anything else
- Regression for direct effects
- Mediation analysis (Process Model 4) for indirect effects through mediators
- Always report effect sizes and confidence intervals, not just p-values

**Dependent variables to consider for behavioural research:** satisfaction, loyalty, negative word-of-mouth intention, behavioural intention, willingness to pay

**Mediators to consider for emotional/social research:** anger/emotion, prior expectations, post-experience responsiveness, perceived control/attribution

#### If Mixed Methods (Both Phases):

Phase 1 is always qualitative. Phase 2 survey is designed from Phase 1 themes. Conceptual model for Phase 2 should be informed by what emerged in interviews, not what was assumed before fieldwork began.

## Document Output Standards

Every research document Alara produces has two potential audiences: an academic reviewer and a business executive. The document must serve both.

### For a Business-Facing Research Document (e.g. Decktopus internal):
Structure:
1. Why We Are Doing This (market shift + business problem as the business case)
2. Research Goals (what we want to understand + research question + sub-questions)
3. What We Will Do With the Findings (specific product, positioning, or strategy decisions this will inform — minimum 4–6 concrete outcomes)
4. Methodology Note (method, participant profile, language, transcription, translation requirements)
5. Opening Script
6. Interview Guide (questions with listening cues and probes)
7. Demographic Questions
8. Interviewer Best Practices

### For an Academic-Facing Research Document (e.g. NOVA IMS):
Structure:
1. Conceptual Model and Research Goal
2. Data Collection Procedures
3. Descriptive Analysis (sample profile, construct statistics, reliability)
4. Main Effect Analysis
5. Mediation/Moderation Results
6. Managerial Insights
7. Annexes (SPSS outputs, frequency histograms, path diagrams)

## Transcription and Translation Standards

All interviews must be:
- Recorded with participant consent (two recording methods recommended)
- Transcribed verbatim — false starts, hedging language, repetitions are data
- Verified manually against the recording — AI transcription tools are a first pass only
- Marked for emotional moments: [laughs], [long pause], [hesitates], [emphatic]
- Translated into English if conducted in another language — faithful translation, not summary. Include original term in brackets where translation is imprecise.
- Anonymised immediately: participants coded as P1, P2 etc. Company names and identifying details removed before team distribution

## Thematic Analysis Standards

- **Inductive, not deductive** — do not start with a coding framework. Read transcripts fully before coding anything.
- **Code for emotion, not just topic** — distinguish between what happened and how the participant felt about it. Emotional codes are where original contribution lives.
- **Look for contradictions** — when a participant says two things that don't fit together, that's the signal, not the noise.
- **Unit of analysis is across participants, not within** — themes must appear in multiple transcripts to count. Single-participant observations are illustrative, not conclusive.
- **Quotes are evidence, not findings** — every quote used in a report must be accompanied by a statement about how many others expressed a similar experience.
- **Map themes to sub-questions** — once themes are stable, connect each to SQ1, SQ2, or SQ3. Themes that don't connect to any sub-question should be flagged as unexpected findings or peripheral observations.

---

## Saturation and Sample Size

- Qualitative: minimum 12, ideal 20–25 participants. Stop when new interviews produce no new themes. Saturation typically occurs between 12–18 for a well-scoped topic.
- Prioritise diversity over volume: 10 interviews across varied contexts beat 20 with similar participants.
- Quantitative: minimum 100 valid responses for regression analysis. 200+ for mediation models with multiple mediators. Clean to 68–70% of raw sample as target.

## Interviewer Best Practices (Always Include in Final Document)

**Before:**
- Know the guide, don't read it
- Test recording equipment in advance — use two methods
- Re-read research goals before each session
- Set up a quiet, private environment

**During:**
- Silence is data — count to five before filling a pause
- Follow the emotion, not just the content — tone shifts are signals
- Never lead — "how did that feel?" not "did that make you anxious?"
- Use the echo technique — repeat the participant's last phrase as a question
- Don't rush — it's fine to skip a lower-priority question if a rich thread is developing
- Track which sub-questions have and haven't surfaced
- Neutral affirmations only — "mmhm," "I see," never "wow" or "exactly"

**After:**
- Write field notes within 30 minutes — demeanour, standout moments, initial themes
- Note non-verbal cues from video/in-person sessions

## Gray Paper Writing Standards

When writing a gray paper to frame a research study:

- Structure as: core tension → phenomenon 1 → phenomenon 2 → phenomenon 3 → the gap this study fills
- Every claim must have a citation with URL
- Claims without sources must be clearly labelled as research hypotheses
- Written for a smart generalist — expert level, accessible language, no unexplained jargon
- End with a key references section with full citation details and live links
- Frame as a business case: why does this matter to the organisation, not just to knowledge

## What Makes Alara's Research Distinctive

From the research conducted to date, Alara's studies share a consistent signature:

- They start with a sharp market observation, not a textbook gap
- They take psychological and consumer behaviour theory seriously as explanatory frameworks (attribution theory, effort heuristic, psychological ownership, cognitive dissonance, impression management)
- They connect academic findings directly to product and positioning decisions
- They are willing to study things the literature hasn't named yet — and to name them
- The qualitative work is genuinely exploratory, not a confirmation exercise
- The quantitative work is methodologically careful — reliability tested, effect sizes reported, mediation models justified

This skill should preserve and extend that signature across all future research projects.

## Reference Studies (Use as Methodological Anchors)

**Hostel Misbehaviour Study (NOVA IMS, 2025) — Quantitative**
- Method: Online survey via Qualtrics, 212 responses, 145 valid after cleaning
- Scale: 26-item Likert (1–5), 5 demographic questions
- Constructs: Consumer Misbehaviour (IV), Anger, Pre/Post Responsiveness, Situation Controllability (mediators), Consumer Satisfaction, Negative WOM Intention, Consumer Loyalty (DVs)
- Analysis: Descriptive statistics, Cronbach's Alpha, simple linear regression, Process Model 4 mediation
- Key finding: Post-responsiveness fully mediated all three outcomes; situation controllability failed as mediator across all three
- Limitation: Consumer Misbehaviour scale had low reliability (α = .182) due to reverse-coded item

**Wearables Study (NOVA IMS, 2025) — Qualitative**
- Method: 25 semi-structured in-depth interviews, 45–60 minutes each
- Analysis: Thematic analysis with LLM-assisted coding, 188 topic clusters across 20 questions, reduced to 4 core themes
- Sample: Purposefully selected for diversity in age, gender, occupation, health goals
- Key contribution: Emotional arc of wearable adoption — expectations, use experience, disengagement — including the paradox that tracking tools sometimes amplified rather than reduced health anxiety

**Decktopus AI Presentation Study (Decktopus, 2026) — Qualitative (Phase 1)**
- Method: Semi-structured in-depth interviews, 15–25 participants, 45–60 minutes each
- Research question: How do professionals navigate the emotional and social dimensions of AI-assisted presentation creation in the post-AI workplace?
- Sub-questions: (1) Ownership and creative identity, (2) Behavioural and emotional patterns through preparation journey, (3) Receiver judgment and feedback loop into creation behaviour
- Key theoretical frameworks: Attribution theory (Heider, 1958), effort heuristic (Kruger et al.), AI Ghostwriter Effect (Draxler et al., 2024), transparency dilemma (Schilke & Reimann, 2025), AI social evaluation penalty (Reif, Larrick & Soll, 2025)
- Original contribution: The "looks AI" visual detection phenomenon and its emotional consequences for creators and receivers — unresearched in existing literature
- Phase 2: Quantitative survey to be designed from interview themes
